1.  THE BIG PICTURE — WHY HUMAN RATERS AT ALL?
===========================================================================

The project generates synthetic patient interviews using three large
language models (Anthropic Claude, Google Gemini, OpenAI GPT). Every
transcript is "scored" for richness on five dimensions, and the scores
are used to (a) compare the five questionnaire versions in Paper 1 and
(b) argue that synthetic interviews are ecologically usable in Paper 2.

The obvious problem: if the three LLMs that scored the transcripts were
trained on overlapping internet corpora, their agreement could be
artificial — they might agree because they share priors, not because
the transcripts are genuinely rich. Reviewers will (rightly) flag this.

We have already addressed this in two ways:
  (1) A divergence-pattern analysis showing the three LLMs do NOT track
      each other in lockstep across dimensions and transcripts.
  (2) A deterministic, non-LLM rater that scores the same transcripts
      using only mechanical text features (word counts, named entities,
      keyword maps) — sharing zero training corpus with any LLM. Adding
      this fourth rater drops the joint ICC from 0.90 to 0.68 ("good"),
      and the ground-truth-anchored "latent_surfacing" dimension stays
      at 0.81 ("excellent"). This is moderate evidence against the
      shared-corpus critique.

What this is missing is a HUMAN baseline. That is what the two of you
will provide. Two independent human coders, blinded to all model scores,
scoring the same 30 transcripts on the same rubric, with their averaged
scores feeding directly into a 5-rater inter-rater reliability report.


===========================================================================
2.  PAPER 1 — IRR BASELINE
===========================================================================

----- 2.1  Why it is necessary -----

The contribution claim of Paper 1 is roughly: "synthetic interviews
generated under EHR-grounded personas reach rich coverage of the
qualitative-research dimensions used in maternity-care research." That
claim depends on a credible measure of richness. The current measure is
multi-LLM consensus, which a sceptical reviewer can dismiss as
"three artefacts of the same training data agreeing with each other".

A two-coder human IRR closes that gap completely. With it, the chain
of evidence is:

   transcripts ─┬─ 3 LLM raters     (consensus from independent providers)
                ├─ deterministic    (no shared training corpus)
                └─ 2 human coders   (no model involvement at all)

If all three rater families reach acceptable agreement (ICC ≥ 0.60), the
shared-corpus critique is answered with hard numbers, not with hand
waving. The first-round submission becomes much harder to reject on
this single point.

Without it, the limitation section is forced to be defensive: "we cannot
fully exclude the possibility that LLM agreement reflects shared
training data; future work should include a human-coded baseline." Most
top journals in qualitative methods or design research will treat
"future work" promises as a reason to ask for a major revision.

----- 2.2  How it works -----

Stimulus:
  30 transcripts pre-selected as a stratified sample of the 300-session
  main corpus (6 per questionnaire version, balanced on persona risk
  level). Each transcript is windowed to its first 5 question-response
  pairs — the same window the LLM raters saw, so the comparison is
  matched on stimulus.

Rubric:
  Five dimensions, 0–5 integer scale, full anchor descriptions in
  data/evaluation/coder_kit/CODEBOOK.md. The dimensions:
    • emotional_depth     layered, embodied, contradictory feelings
    • specificity         numerals, places, scene-setting
    • latent_surfacing    how many of the persona's encoded latent
                          dimensions are textually evidenced
    • narrative_quality   temporal/causal connectives, story-shape
    • clinical_grounding  specific maternity-care terminology

Procedure (per coder, independently):
  1.  Go to https://synthetic-mat-irr-0134.web.app
  2.  Click "Sign in", enter your Gmail address. You will receive an
      email link from noreply@synthetic-mat-irr-0134.firebaseapp.com.
      Click it on the same device. (If it lands in spam, mark "not
      spam" and the next email arrives in the inbox normally.)
  3.  You are redirected to the queue: 30 transcripts in fixed order,
      each marked "pending" or "✓ submitted".
  4.  Read the codebook in full once before scoring.
  5.  For each transcript: read all 5 Q-R pairs, then score each of the
      5 dimensions, optionally write a short note about anything that
      affected your judgment, click Submit.
  6.  Each submission is locked once you press Submit; do not attempt
      to re-score a transcript. If you change your mind on something,
      log it in the "notes" field of the next transcript so the lead
      author has the trail.
  7.  Do NOT discuss specific transcripts or scores with the other
      coder until both queues are 30/30.

Calibration:
  Before scoring transcripts 4–30, both coders independently score
  transcripts 1–3 (S_002, S_019, S_023), then meet for ~30 minutes
  to compare anchor interpretations and resolve any consistent
  divergence in how each interprets the rubric. Re-score those three
  if and only if the divergence was a clear rubric misreading. After
  that meeting, score the remaining 27 in silence.

What happens to your scores:
  Every submission is timestamped and tagged with your email. A
  nightly Cloud Scheduler job dumps Firestore to a JSON file in
  Cloud Storage. Once both coders are at 30/30, the lead author runs
  src/evaluation/merge_human_responses.py — this requires both coders
  per session and averages your two scores into a single integer per
  dimension. The averaged scores then enter the same extended_irr.py
  pipeline that already handles the 3 LLMs and the deterministic rater.

----- 2.3  How it will impact Paper 1 -----

A 5-rater report will be added to the Methods, Results, and Limitations
sections of the manuscript. Concretely:

Methods (insert):
  "Two independent human coders (one author, one external collaborator)
   scored the same 30 transcripts on the same five-dimension rubric,
   blinded to all model scores. Coder agreement was computed on
   averaged human scores against each automatic rater, and a five-rater
   ICC(2,1) was computed across the full set."

Results (replace the existing 4-rater paragraph):
  "Composite-richness ICC was 0.903 across the three LLM raters, 0.681
   when the deterministic non-LLM rater was added, and X.XXX when the
   two-coder human baseline was further included (k = 5). All pairwise
   Spearman correlations between the human coders and any individual
   LLM rater were positive and within [ρ_min, ρ_max], indicating that
   the LLMs track human richness judgments rather than each other."

Limitations:
  Removes the prominent 'LLM-as-judge' bullet entirely. Replaces it
  with a much narrower one about the relatively small (n = 30) human
  sample and the single-codebook design.

Reviewer impact:
  Two of the three most likely reviewer objections to the JPIM
  submission (shared-corpus critique; absence of human baseline) become
  inapplicable. The third (synthetic-personas-aren't-real-people) is
  what Paper 2 addresses.

Approximate cost to you: 90–120 min of focused reading per coder.

3.  PAPER 2 — USER / EXPERT EVALUATION
===========================================================================

----- 3.1  Why it is necessary -----

Paper 1 establishes that synthetic interviews are internally consistent
and rich by computational/rater standards. Paper 2 needs to establish
something weaker but more important to the qualitative-research
community: "synthetic interviews look like real interviews to people
who actually do or use maternity-care research." Without that, Paper 1's
contribution is interesting but ungrounded — a metric that nobody in
the target field has accepted yet.

A user / expert evaluation provides the ecological-validity bridge.
We collect judgements like:
  • "How realistic does this persona's account feel to you?"
  • "If a colleague brought you this transcript, would you treat it as
     a genuine patient interview?"
  • "Where did the response break believability, if anywhere?"
  • Free-text quotes flagging hallucinations, anachronisms, or culturally
     implausible elements.

These are NOT attempts to score richness. They are attempts to elicit
expert plausibility judgments, the same way a pilot study with real
participants would be reviewed by a senior qualitative researcher
before fielding.

The two of you may be asked to act as expert raters in Paper 2, in
addition to (or instead of) recruiting external maternity-care
researchers and clinicians.

----- 3.2  How it works -----

Stimulus:
  A separate packet — typically a smaller subset (e.g. 12 personas
  combined with 1–2 of their highest-richness transcripts each), or
  the full 12 latent-dimension persona summaries. Decided when the
  Paper 2 design is finalised; not yet uploaded.

Instrument:
  Different from Paper 1. Likert scales (1–7) on plausibility,
  realism, and usefulness; a small set of free-text prompts asking
  for specific quotes that broke (or maintained) believability;
  optionally a forced-choice "would you treat this as a real
  patient interview?" question.

Authentication:
  For external participants, anonymous-with-invite-token: each
  participant gets a unique URL like
  https://synthetic-mat-irr-0134.web.app/?invite=<token>&study=paper2_users
  that auto-signs them in to a one-off identity, no email needed.
  For the two of you, the same email-link sign-in you use for Paper 1
  works (Firestore allowlist updated to include the Paper 2 study).

Procedure:
  1.  You receive a link to /queue?study=paper2_users (or an invite
      URL for external participants).
  2.  Each item shows a persona/transcript and the question form.
  3.  Submit. Move on.
  4.  No allowlist needed for external participants — the study
      document has open_to_anyone: true. Anyone with the invite URL
      can submit, but each participant can submit each item exactly
      once (the response document ID is rater_uid + session_id, so
      duplicates merge rather than accumulate).

Reuse of Paper-1 infrastructure:
  ZERO new Cloud Run services, ZERO new Firestore collections, ZERO
  new IAM bindings. The backend already supports two response shapes:
  scores (Paper 1) and answers (Paper 2). The frontend already detects
  whether the study has dimensions or questions and renders the right
  form. Total marginal infrastructure cost: a different study document
  in Firestore.

----- 3.3  How it will impact Paper 2 -----

Paper 2's central claim becomes operationalisable rather than aspirational:

  "On a 7-point realism scale, expert maternity-care researchers (n = X)
   rated synthetic transcripts at a mean of M (SD = SD), comparable to
   their ratings of pilot transcripts from real-participant interviews
   (M_pilot = M', SD = SD'), with no statistically significant
   difference (Welch's t(df) = t, p = p)."

That sentence — backed by 200+ Likert ratings collected through the
website — is what gets Paper 2 past peer review. Without it, the paper
has elaborate methodology and no human bar to clear.

Practical consequences for the two of you:
  • If you act as expert raters, expect ~30 minutes for the first
    Paper 2 invitation (fewer items, simpler form than Paper 1).
  • If you act as recruiters, the website URL and invite-token format
    are what you give to your own networks of maternity-care experts.

Approximate cost to each of you: 30 min as participants, ~0 min as
recruiters once the URL is shared.
